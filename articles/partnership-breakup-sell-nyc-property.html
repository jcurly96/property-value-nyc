Partnership Breaking Up? Selling a Jointly Owned NYC Property

Co-owning an NYC property with a business partner works well until it doesn't. When partners' goals diverge — or a falling-out unrelated to the building itself makes continued co-ownership untenable — the question of what to do with a jointly owned property becomes urgent and often emotionally charged. Understanding your options ahead of time makes it far easier to navigate a partnership breakup without it turning into a protracted, expensive dispute.

Why Partnerships Split Over Real Estate

Partnership breakups involving jointly owned property usually trace back to one of a few common causes. Sometimes it's purely financial: one partner needs liquidity now — for another investment, a personal need, or simply a change in risk tolerance — while the other wants to keep holding for long-term appreciation or income. Other times, partners' broader business relationship has soured for reasons that have nothing to do with the property itself, and continuing to co-own real estate together becomes impossible to separate from a relationship neither side wants to continue. Either way, the property often ends up as the most complicated asset to untangle.

It's also common for partners to simply drift apart in how actively they want to be involved. One partner may want to stay hands-on, making day-to-day decisions and pursuing capital improvements, while the other has quietly become a passive investor in practice, uninterested in the operational details but still nominally an equal decision-maker. That imbalance, left unaddressed, tends to surface eventually — often at the worst possible time, like a refinancing decision or a major capital expense.

Review Your Partnership Agreement First

Before any conversation about price or process, revisit the partnership or operating agreement that governs the property. Most well-drafted agreements include exit mechanics specifically designed for this situation: buy-sell provisions that establish how one partner can buy out another, rights of first refusal (ROFR) that give existing partners the option to match a third-party offer before it goes to an outside buyer, and sometimes a predetermined valuation methodology or appraisal process for exactly this scenario.

These provisions exist precisely because partnership breakups are common enough that experienced attorneys build exit ramps into the original agreement. Reviewing this document early — ideally with your attorney — tells you what's actually possible procedurally before you and your partner start negotiating from assumptions that may not match what you actually agreed to years ago.

Why a Neutral Valuation Is Essential

In almost every buyout scenario, both partners have an obvious financial incentive to argue for a number that favors them — the partner buying out wants a lower price, the partner selling their stake wants a higher one. Relying on either partner's own estimate, or a number pulled from an old refinance appraisal, tends to breed distrust and stall negotiations.

An independent, third-party valuation gives both partners the same starting point and removes the perception that either side is negotiating from self-interest — often the single fastest way to move a stalled buyout forward.

A current, professional valuation based on the property's actual income, expenses, and comparable market activity gives both sides a defensible, neutral reference point. Even when partners ultimately negotiate away from that number for their own reasons, having it on the table tends to keep the conversation grounded and move things forward faster.

When Neither Partner Wants the Property

Not every partnership breakup ends in one partner buying out the other. Frequently, neither side wants to continue owning the building — one wants out and the other doesn't want the financial or management burden of taking full ownership either. In that case, a clean sale to a third party, with proceeds split according to ownership percentages, is often the simplest resolution. It avoids the complexity of financing a buyout, ends any risk of ongoing disputes over a shared asset, and lets both partners fully exit and move on without lingering financial ties to each other.

A third-party sale also sidesteps a problem that buyouts often run into: financing. Buying out a partner typically requires the remaining owner to either have significant liquid capital on hand or qualify for new financing on their own, sometimes on short notice. When that financing isn't readily available, insisting on a buyout can stall the whole process for months. A sale to an outside buyer removes that bottleneck entirely.

Tax and Legal Considerations for Departing Partners

How a property is titled and how the partnership is structured — a general partnership, an LLC, a tenancy-in-common arrangement — affects both the mechanics and the tax consequences of a buyout or sale. A partner selling their interest may have options worth exploring with a CPA, such as a 1031 exchange to defer capital gains on their share of the proceeds if they plan to reinvest in other real estate. These structures and their tax treatment vary considerably, so it's worth involving your attorney and CPA before, not after, terms are finalized.

Moving Forward Without Unnecessary Conflict

Partnership breakups are stressful enough without a property adding fuel to the fire. Getting an independent valuation early, reviewing your governing agreement, and being clear-eyed about whether a buyout or a third-party sale better serves both partners can turn a potentially contentious process into a straightforward transaction. As with any partnership dissolution, your attorney and CPA should review the tax and legal implications specific to your ownership structure before finalizing anything.
