Assemblage Sales: When a Neighboring Owner Wants to Combine Lots

If a developer has approached you about buying your building alongside one or more of your neighbors' properties, you're being offered a role in what's known as an assemblage sale — and it can be one of the more lucrative positions an NYC owner can be in, if you understand your leverage. Combining adjacent lots into a single development site can unlock far more value than any one parcel could generate on its own, which means the owner of a needed piece can often command a real premium over standalone land value. Here's how to think it through.

What Assemblage Actually Means

Assemblage is the process of combining two or more adjacent tax lots into a single zoning lot, allowing a developer to build a larger, more efficient project than any individual site could support on its own. Zoning bulk, height, and floor area rules generally reward larger, more contiguous sites — a combined lot can often support a more efficient building footprint, better unit layouts, and stronger overall economics than the same square footage split across several small, separately developed parcels. That's the entire premise behind why a developer would go through the trouble of negotiating with multiple owners instead of just building on whatever they already control.

Why Assemblage Sites Often Command a Premium

The economics of assemblage create a specific kind of leverage for the owner of a needed parcel. If a developer has already spent time, capital, and negotiating effort assembling several of the lots in a planned site, the last piece of the puzzle can become disproportionately valuable — the deal simply doesn't work at full scale without it. An owner in that position isn't just selling standalone land; they're selling the ability to unlock a project the developer may already be significantly invested in pursuing. That dynamic is exactly why assemblage parcels can command pricing well above what the same lot would fetch as an isolated sale.

The value of an assemblage parcel isn't just what the land is worth on its own — it's what the whole combined site becomes with your parcel in it, versus without it.

Risks Worth Understanding Before You Engage

Assemblage negotiations come with real tradeoffs an owner should go in aware of:

  • Deals can stall for a long time. If the developer is still waiting on other parcels, financing, or approvals to fall into place, a deal that looked imminent can drag on for months or longer while your property sits effectively off the market.
  • Confidentiality is often expected. Assembling developers frequently ask owners not to disclose the broader assemblage plan, since revealing it can drive up pricing from other parcel owners once they realize they're part of a larger play.
  • Timeline needs to be understood upfront. Before tying up your property in an assemblage negotiation, it's worth knowing realistically how contingent the deal is on other pieces falling into place — an owner who needs certainty on a shorter timeline may be better served exploring other options.

Evaluating Whether the Offer Reflects Your Real Share

The hardest part of an assemblage negotiation is figuring out whether the number on the table genuinely reflects a fair share of the value that combining the lots unlocks — or whether it's really just standalone site value with a modest bump attached to make it sound more compelling. A developer benefits from an owner who anchors to their individual parcel's standalone worth rather than to what the assembled site as a whole is likely to be worth once built out. Getting a real read on both numbers — what your site is worth alone, and what a reasonable allocation of the combined site's development value would look like — is the only way to know where a given offer actually falls.

Going In With Your Own Number

Owners who enter assemblage negotiations with an independent understanding of their property's standalone value, and a sense of the site's development potential more broadly, are in a far stronger position than owners negotiating purely off the developer's framing. That's true whether you ultimately decide to participate, hold out, or walk away entirely.
